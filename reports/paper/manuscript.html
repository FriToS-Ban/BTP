Furnace cooling versus air cooling of carbon and low-alloy steels, and the rest of the heat-treatment route: a physics-anchored, uncertainty-aware prediction of microstructure and tensile properties

Aviral Singh, Shibasish Banerjee, Anish Karmakar*
Department of Metallurgical and Materials Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee, Roorkee 247667, India. * Corresponding author.

Highlights

  • An open chain from composition and cooling route to microstructure and tensile properties, with calibrated 90 % intervals.
  • SAE hardenability bands of 80 steels show the Li et al. kinetics are too hardenable away from the quenched end.
  • A cross-validated Bayesian recalibration of the bainite reaction transfers to independent dilatometry and a CCT atlas.
  • Physics carries extrapolation to new cooling rates; machine learning corrects the textbook structure-property laws.
  • Air cooling refines plain-carbon steels but turns hardenable Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo bars partly bainitic.
  • Hardening, tempering and stress relieving in the same chain: quench heat flow, a Hollomon-Jaffe tempering model, 75 MPa on unseen grades.
  • A documented bar size replaced an inferred one, and an MCMC posterior shows the kinetic errors are structural, not parametric.

Abstract

Full annealing (furnace cooling) and normalising (still-air cooling) are the two most common heat treatments of carbon and low-alloy steels, yet their outcome for an arbitrary composition and bar size is usually read from handbook tables for a few grades. We present an open, end-to-end model that predicts, from the composition, the steel category, the microstructure after either route (ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite fractions, pearlite spacing, ferrite grain size) and the yield strength, tensile strength, uniform and total elongation, hardness and impact transition temperature, each with a calibrated 90 % interval. The chain combines empirical thermodynamics, a heat balance of the cooling bar, the Kirkaldy-Venugopalan / Li et al. transformation kinetics, the Gladman-McIvor-Pickering and Maynier structure-property laws, and Gaussian-process models that learn a multiplicative correction to the physics. Against the SAE end-quench hardenability bands of 80 steels the published kinetics were too hardenable away from the quenched end; a Bayesian recalibration of the bainite reaction, cross-validated by grade, reduced the band error from 10.1 to 5.9 HRC and moved the predicted bainite start onto independent dilatometry. On grades unseen in training the model predicts tensile strength within 47 MPa, yield strength within 44 MPa and hardness within 16 HB (RMSE), against 94, 100 and 23 for the textbook laws alone, and it keeps physically consistent trends at cooling rates absent from the training data. Applied to 271 standard grades, it shows that air cooling only refines low-carbon steels, raises the strength of medium- and high-carbon steels through finer pearlite and ferrite, and gives hardenable Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo bars a partly bainitic structure, so that for them bar size matters as much as the route. The chain is extended to hardening (radial quench heat flow with Grossmann's severity), tempering (a Hollomon-Jaffe master curve with secondary hardening, fitted on 324 quenched-and-tempered bars of 28 grades at four bar sizes) and stress relieving. It predicts the tensile strength of quenched-and-tempered bars of unseen grades within 75 MPa (textbook physics alone: 247 MPa) and reproduces the mass effect. An explicit register of assumptions corrected the bar size of the handbook's normalised data (25 mm, documented, instead of an inferred 50 mm). A Markov-chain Monte Carlo posterior of the kinetic constants, propagated to an independent CCT atlas, shows that the transformation model's errors are structural rather than parametric.

Keywords: normalising; full annealing; continuous cooling transformation; hardenability; Jominy; physics-informed machine learning; Gaussian process; conformal prediction

1. Introduction

Whether a steel bar is cooled with the furnace or in still air decides how its austenite transforms, and therefore its strength, ductility and toughness. Air cooling transforms the austenite at a larger undercooling, so the pearlite is finer and the ferrite grains are smaller (Zener 1946; Marder and Bramfitt 1976); in hypoeutectoid steels the austenite may enter the extrapolated Hultgren region before all the equilibrium ferrite has formed; and in steels of sufficient hardenability the ferrite and pearlite reactions are delayed so much that bainite or martensite forms in an air-cooled bar (Krauss 2015; Bhadeshia and Honeycombe 2017). The two routes also differ in austenitising temperature, and the outcome depends on the bar size through its cooling rate (the mass effect).

Two families of models address such questions. Semi-empirical kinetics with an additivity rule (Kirkaldy and Venugopalan 1984; Li et al. 1998), with an open implementation by Collins et al. (2023), predict the constituents for arbitrary cooling paths, and commercial tools combine CALPHAD thermodynamics with kinetic and structure-property models (Saunders et al. 2003). Data-driven models predict CCT or TTT behaviour or properties directly from composition and processing (Bhadeshia 1999; Trzaska and Dobrzański 2007; Agrawal et al. 2014; Xiong et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2020), increasingly with physical constraints or features (Shen et al. 2019; Cao et al. 2023; Hedström et al. 2026). With small data their main risks are extrapolation and over-confidence (Meredig et al. 2018; Sutton et al. 2020).

Here we combine the two in an open chain built specifically for the furnace-versus-air question, with three aims: (i) to answer it quantitatively for any carbon or low-alloy composition and bar size, (ii) to test every link of the chain on measured data it was not trained on, and (iii) to let physics carry the extrapolation while machine learning corrects what the textbook relations miss, with honest intervals. We test five expectations from the literature: finer pearlite and ferrite on air cooling (H1), a gain that grows with the pearlite share (H2), bainite or martensite in air-cooled hardenable steels (H3), a mass effect (H4), and more pearlite after normalising than after annealing (H5).

2. Data

All data are open or factual handbook values reproduced openly (Table 1). The property models learn only from typical values of 33 AISI grades in the annealed and normalised conditions (ASM Handbook Vol. 1, data of Bethlehem Steel); all other data serve as tests. Handbook typical values and specification minima were never mixed. Composition-table errors in the sources were corrected and logged. For the literature compilation every transcribed number was checked by script against the saved paper, and 51 implausible values were flagged and excluded from all metrics.

3. Methods

3.1 Steel category

Five classifiers were trained on 21,880 heats simulated inside the composition ranges of 547 grades and evaluated by grade-grouped cross-validation and on real heats; the production model (XGBoost) was selected by a rule fixed in advance (best accuracy on real heats). The AISI/SAE composition limits, written as a decision list with nothing fitted, serve as the baseline. A novelty score (distance to the nearest grade centroid) flags compositions outside every category.

3.2 Cooling paths and critical temperatures

Furnace cooling is Newtonian at 0.005 K/s at 700 °C (18 K/h). Still-air cooling is a lumped heat balance of a long round bar with natural convection, radiation (ε = 0.8) and the EN 1993-1-2 heat capacity; it gives mean 800-500 °C rates of 0.90 and 0.43 K/s for 25 and 50 mm bars, against 0.89 and 0.36-0.46 K/s measured for comparable bars. The Jominy bar is solved by 1-D conduction, with a water-jet coefficient of 8000 W m⁻² K⁻¹ fitted to the cooling-rate scale of the ASM hardenability charts (agreement within a factor of 0.86-1.26). Ae1 and Ae3 follow Grange (1961) and Acm the fit used by Collins et al.; the equations are flagged outside C ≤ 1.05, Si ≤ 1.0 % (above which Grange's silicon terms move the eutectoid carbon the wrong way), and above the eutectoid the upper critical temperature is drawn with the binary Fe-Fe₃C solvus slope. Default austenitising temperatures follow the practice of the training data: annealing at max(Ae3 + 50, Ae1 + 65) °C and normalising at the upper critical temperature + 110 °C, but not below 870 °C (Fig. 1).

3.3 Transformation kinetics and their Bayesian calibration

The Kirkaldy-Venugopalan model as modified by Li et al. (1998) is integrated along each cooling path with Scheil's additivity rule; ferrite grows below Ae3 up to the extrapolated-Ae3 limit, pearlite starts once the enriched austenite lies below its own Ae3 and Acm (Hultgren condition), bainite forms below Bs and martensite follows Koistinen and Marburger (1959). Our implementation reproduces the authors' code to 1-2 °C. Four quantities were then calibrated on the SAE H-bands of the non-boron H-steels (1040 band points): multipliers on the ferrite, pearlite and bainite rate constants and a shift ΔBs of the bainite start. The published model is the prior (log-multipliers ~ N(0, 1), ΔBs ~ N(0, 50 °C)); the middle of each band is the target with a standard deviation of a quarter of the band width plus a fitted model error; the maximum a posteriori estimate is found by Nelder-Mead from several starts, with a Laplace approximation of its uncertainty. The bands left the ferrite and pearlite rates at their published values and lowered Bs by 107 °C with a faster bainite reaction below it (factor 79). The calibration was cross-validated by grade (5 folds) and adopted only after testing it on data it did not see (Section 4.1).

3.4 Microstructure quantities

The kinetics give the bainite, martensite and proeutectoid cementite fractions; the ferrite + pearlite is reported with its lever-rule split as the central value and the kinetic split as the upper end of a band (Section 4.2). The pearlite spacing is S₀ = 3.33 ΔT^−0.607 µm, fitted on 14 measured spacings; the ferrite grain size uses the low-Ceq branch of Hodgson and Gibbs (1992) with Ceq ≤ 0.35, which removed an unphysical 3 µm floor for medium-carbon steels and reduced the typical error on 20 measured grains from a factor of 2.7 to 2.1.

3.5 Property models

Models were compared by leave-one-grade-out cross-validation on the 80 handbook rows: the textbook laws (Gladman, McIvor and Pickering 1972 for ferrite + pearlite; Maynier et al. 1978 for bainite and martensite); ridge, gradient boosting and Gaussian processes (GP) on composition and on physics descriptors; the textbook laws with a GP residual; a grey box with learned, sign-constrained Gladman coefficients; and physics-anchored GPs that scale each property on the physics strength UTSphys (Gladman law on the predicted ferrite fraction, grain size and spacing; Maynier hardness for bainite and martensite) and learn only a multiplicative correction, which fades to the physics far from the data (a discrepancy model in the sense of Kennedy and O'Hagan 2001). The production model averages the physics-anchored chain, the textbook laws with a GP residual and a GP on composition and descriptors; for more martensite or bainite + martensite than any training steel, the weight of the purely data-driven member moves to the physics-anchored ones. The microstructure enters the ML through the Gladman mixture terms, and silicon and vanadium enter only through the physics (every training grade but one has the same nominal silicon).

3.6 Uncertainty, consistency and evaluation

Ninety per cent intervals are normalised split-conformal intervals (Lei et al. 2018) with coverage checked by leave-one-grade-out. Paired comparisons use per-grade errors, Wilcoxon tests with the Holm adjustment and bootstrap intervals. Physical consistency is checked on sweeps absent from the data (cooling rate, carbon, silicon). Uniform elongation is estimated as a fraction (0.508 ± 0.051) of the total elongation after converting all tests to one gauge geometry with Oliver's relation (ISO 2566-1); toughness is reported as Pickering's (1978) impact transition temperature. Design choices made after seeing evaluation results are documented as ablations.

4. Results

4.1 Transformation kinetics

Against dilatometry of three steels the model predicts the ferrite start within 15.7 °C, the pearlite start within 22.6 °C and Ms within 7.4 °C (MAE). Against 423 measured CCT points of 19 steels it predicts the ferrite + pearlite fraction within 0.12, bainite within 0.16 and martensite within 0.12 (MAE) and hardness within 68 HV, with larger errors at air-cooling times (ferrite + pearlite 0.14) than for quench-like cooling (0.05). Against the H-bands the published constants are right at the quenched end (J1-J4 bias +0.6 HRC) but too hardenable along the bar (J5-J16 +7.7 HRC, J18-J32 +12.3 HRC, 17 % of points inside the band); the bainite start measured by dilatometry was +67 °C below the published value, and air-cooled 4140 in the literature was softer than predicted: three independent signs of one problem. The calibration reduced the band error on unseen grades from 10.1 to 5.9 HRC (54 % → 78 % inside the band; Fig. 2) and transferred to independent data (Table 2), with one exception, SA-540 (3.5Ni-1.7Cr-0.5Mo), richer than any H-grade, which it makes too bainitic at 0.5-1 K/s; we adopt it with that stated limit.

4.2 Microstructure

Figure 4 shows the model CCT diagrams of a 1045 and a 4140 steel with both cooling paths: the same still-air path gives ferrite + pearlite in the 1045 and bainite in the 4140. For furnace-cooled steels both the kinetics and the lever rule agree with measured pearlite fractions (MAE 4.3 and 5.5 points). For normalised steels the kinetics reproduce the quasi-eutectoid excess of pearlite (H5) but overstate it (MAE 19.0 against 12.4 points for the lever rule), and the measured image-analysis fractions lie at or below the lever value, while an older regression for hot-rolled and normalised low-carbon steels indicates a substantial excess; we therefore report a band and regard H5 as quantitatively unsettled. Normalising refines the ferrite from 38 to 18 µm in low-carbon and from 19 to 12 µm in medium-carbon steels (25 mm bar). Published micrographs of 1045 and 4140 after both routes (Fig. 5) agree with the predicted constituents, the normalised 4140 showing the fine acicular structure of the predicted bainite.

The production model predicts UTS within 47 MPa (R² 0.94) and hardness within 16 HB (R² 0.92), clearly better than the textbook laws (Holm-adjusted p = 0.001 for UTS) and statistically tied, inside the data, with a composition-only GP (difference 16 MPa, 95 % interval 2 to 30). Its advantage lies outside the data: at cooling rates of 0.1-5 K/s that the training data do not contain it predicts the dilatometry hardness within 68 HB, against 57 HB for the textbook laws and 109 HB for the composition GP, which cannot produce martensite hardness. Anchoring on the Gladman law instead of the Maynier hardness cut the dilatometry hardness error from 73 to 68 HB and the out-of-domain UTS error from 114 to 108 MPa, while the in-domain UTS error changed from 48 to 47 MPa and elongation from 2.6 to 2.6 % (`results/s04_anchor_ablation.csv`). Its UTS moves the wrong way in only 0.5 % of the steps of the cooling-rate sweeps and changes by 26 MPa over 0.10-0.40 % Si (with silicon as an ML input: 264 MPa). Varying each training grade's composition within its own specification range moves the predicted UTS by 18 MPa (furnace) and 59 MPa (air, RMS), so a large part of the remaining error reflects the unknown composition of the tested heats. The 90 % intervals cover 92.5 % of unseen grades.

4.4 External validation

For 165 hot-rolled structural members the UTS is predicted within 68 MPa (bias -60) and the elongation within 3.0 %, while the yield strength is 77 MPa too low (bias 102 MPa for microalloyed members), as expected for modern thermomechanically rolled sections that the handbook bars do not represent; the predicted transition temperature ranks their Charpy energy with the correct sign (Spearman -0.25). On unflagged literature values the yield strength is 28 MPa and the UTS 55 MPa off (MAE), and for steels tested after both routes the predicted direction of change is always correct.

Low-carbon steels keep ferrite with about 15 % pearlite after both routes; air cooling only refines them, and normalising lowers the predicted transition temperature by about 26 °C, consistent with its use for grain refinement (H1). In medium- and high-carbon steels the gain grows with the pearlite share, about 79 and 250 MPa for a 25 mm bar (H2; Fig. 6). Hardenable Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo steels become partly bainitic in air-cooled bars (H3); the median Cr-Mo grade gains 117 MPa. Separating the two differences between the routes shows that almost all of the gain comes from the faster cooling (medium carbon: +84 MPa) rather than from the hotter austenitisation (-4 MPa). Grossmann's ideal critical diameter, computed independently of the kinetics, orders the air-cooling response of all grades (Fig. 7), and for hardenable steels the bar size decides between martensite, bainite and ferrite + pearlite (H4).

4.6 Hardening, tempering and stress relieving

Quench paths come from transient radial conduction in the bar with Grossmann's severity H (h = 2kH, k = 30 W m-1 K-1), verified against the exact Bessel-series solution; the specific-heat peak of slow transformation is removed for a quench. Against Grossmann's DI (ASTM A255), which does not use the kinetics, the model's ideal critical diameter is 1.56 times too large (median; 2.53 with the published constants), so the kinetics remain too hardenable, most of all for high-carbon steels. Martensite tempers along a logistic master curve of P = T(20 + log t) with alloy-dependent delay and a secondary-hardening term; fitted on the 324 bars, molybdenum delays softening by about 40 °C per 0.1 % at 600 °C, and the fully tempered hardness rises by only 90 HV per % C against about 950 for untempered martensite: carbon's effect fades with tempering, as Grange, Hribal and Porter found. In leave-one-grade-out tests the textbook chain errs by 247 MPa, gradient boosting by 112 MPa, a GP by 91 MPa and the production mixture of a physics-anchored GP and a GP by 75 MPa (yield strength 85 MPa, elongation 1.7 %), with nested 90 % coverage of 89 % and no bias with bar size (Fig. 9). On these bars, which played no part in the calibration, the recalibrated kinetics are neither clearly better nor worse (grey box 99 against 96 MPa, physics-anchored GP 86 against 93 MPa), because strength after tempering at 480-650 °C is insensitive to the bainite share. A stress relief bounds the residual stress by the Eurocode yield reduction at temperature and changes the hardness of a tempered steel only above its tempering parameter (4140 tempered at 600 °C: 991 MPa after a 575 °C relief, 916 MPa after 650 °C). Precipitation strengthening of V and Nb is estimated from solubility products and Ashby-Orowan; Charpy values of tempered steel are declared out of scope, with embrittlement windows flagged.

4.7 Assumptions and kinetic uncertainty

All conditions the models rely on are registered with their status and range. The bar size of the handbook's normalised data, inferred as 50 mm in earlier versions from the physics-only error, is documented in the Birbilis et al. database: the 25 mm bars match within 3 MPa for most grades, the 50 mm bars are on average 29 MPa weaker. Retraining over the range of every remaining assumption leaves the furnace-versus-air conclusions unchanged (Fig. 10). The kinetic posterior was sampled with four adaptive Metropolis chains (split R-hat ≤ 1.007); propagated to the 423 points of the CCT atlas, the parameter uncertainty covers only 60 % of the measured ferrite + pearlite fractions, and a structural error of about 0.24 in phase fraction is needed for nominal coverage. The model's remaining error is in its form, not its constants.

5. Discussion

The physics in the chain does not buy much accuracy where data exist: inside the training domain a composition-only GP is as accurate. It buys three things the data alone cannot give: predictions at cooling rates and microstructures absent from the training set, physically consistent trends with cooling rate, carbon and silicon, and an explanation of each prediction in metallurgical terms. Two lessons generalise. First, cross-validation could not detect a spurious silicon effect or an anchor blind to pearlite refinement; both were found only by sweeping one input at a time. Second, a calibration that fits one kind of data must be tested on others: a hardness-only calibration can trade errors between mechanisms, and here the bainite calibration was accepted because it moved the bainite start onto independent dilatometry and improved independent hardness and fraction data. The main limitations are the small and old training sets (33 handbook grades and 28 quenched-and-tempered grades at nominal compositions, with one inferred furnace rate), the structural error of the semi-empirical kinetics, Grossmann's constant heat-transfer coefficient for the quench, the estimated nature of precipitation strengthening, uniform elongation, grain size and toughness, and the domain shift to modern rolled products, which the tool now detects and declines.

6. Conclusions

  • For low-carbon steels furnace and air cooling give almost the same steel; air cooling refines ferrite and pearlite and lowers the transition temperature.
  • For medium- and high-carbon steels air cooling gives the same phases with finer pearlite and ferrite, and a strength gain that grows with the pearlite share.
  • For hardenable Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo steels air cooling gives a partly bainitic steel, so route and bar size must be chosen together; hardenability (DI) orders this response.
  • The published Li et al. kinetics are too hardenable away from the quenched end; a bainite recalibration on 80 H-bands cross-validates and transfers to independent data.
  • Anchoring machine learning on microstructure-based physics gives accuracy comparable to the best data-driven model inside the data and much better behaviour outside it, with calibrated intervals.
  • Hardening and tempering follow from the same kinetics with a quench heat flow and a fitted Hollomon-Jaffe master curve; quenched-and-tempered strength is predicted within 75 MPa on unseen grades across 13-100 mm bars.
  • Making every assumption explicit corrected one of them, and a proper posterior showed that the remaining kinetic error is structural: better equations, not more calibration data of the same kind, are the way forward.

Appendix: pre-registered experimental validation protocol

Predictions for eight steels available in India (EN3B, EN8, EN9, EN18, EN19, EN24, EN42 and an 8620-type), furnace cooled and air cooled as 10 and 25 mm bars, were recorded with their intervals before any test (results/s07_lab_plan_preregistered.csv). The protocol specifies spark-OES chemistry of the actual bars (with predictions re-issued for it), thermocouple records of the real cooling curves, tensile tests with an extensometer, Brinell or Vickers hardness below the decarburised layer, point counting (ASTM E562), grain size (ASTM E112) and SEM pearlite spacing.
