Introduction

What This Project Does

This project is about predicting what happens inside a carbon or low-alloy steel when it is heat treated, and what properties come out of it at the end. We built an open model that takes 3 inputs, the chemical composition of the steel, the heat treatment given to it and the size of the bar, and from these it predicts the phases that form, how fine they are, and the yield strength, tensile strength, elongation and hardness of the bar, each with a 90% interval. It covers the 5 classic heat treatments, which are annealing, normalising, hardening, tempering and stress relieving, so a single model can answer the everyday question of a heat-treatment shop, which is simply what this steel will be like after this treatment.

The work started from one question set by our supervisor, which is how furnace cooling and air cooling change a steel. These 2 routes are the textbook difference between annealing and normalising, and answering the question properly needs the whole chain, from the cooling of the bar to the transformation of the austenite to the strength that the final microstructure gives. Once that chain was in place it was natural to extend it to the rest of the route, so the same physics now also handles quenching, tempering and stress relieving.

What makes the model useful, we think, is the way it is built. Every step follows the metallurgy, so the physics carries the prediction into conditions that are not in the data, and machine learning is used only to correct what the textbook relations get wrong. Every error quoted in this report was measured on steels that the model never saw during training, and every prediction comes with an interval that we checked really holds on such steels. The whole study can be run again from one command, so anyone can reproduce it or build on it.

Heat Treatment of Steels

Most steel parts are heat treated before they go into service, because the same steel can be made soft and ductile or hard and strong just by changing how it is heated and cooled. Heating above the upper critical temperature turns the steel into austenite, and what the austenite transforms into on cooling depends on how fast it is cooled, on the composition of the steel and on the size of the part . Slow cooling gives ferrite and pearlite, faster cooling gives finer pearlite and then bainite, and a quench gives martensite, which is very hard but also brittle until it is tempered.

The size of the effect is quite large. AISI 4140 has a typical tensile strength of about 655 MPa when it is annealed and about 1020 MPa when it is normalised as a 25 mm bar , and when a 25 mm bar is quenched in oil and tempered, the measured values run from about 920 MPa after tempering at 650 °C to about 1800 MPa after tempering at 205 °C . So the heat treatment can easily matter as much as the choice of the steel itself, and a model that ignores the heat treatment cannot say much that is useful about strength.

Motivation

In industry the properties of a heat-treated part are usually found by experience and by testing, and handbooks give typical values for only a few standard conditions, often one bar size and one treatment per grade. A designer who needs a 60 mm bar instead of a 25 mm one, or a different tempering temperature, has to guess or has to test. Commercial software such as JMatPro does predict transformations and properties , but it is closed and expensive, and its predictions cannot be checked line by line.

Machine learning looks like a quick answer, but it has 2 real problems with steel. Most open datasets give the composition and the strength without saying how the steel was heat treated, so 2 steels with the same chemistry and different treatments look exactly the same to the model. Also, a model trained on a few dozen grades does not know how to extrapolate, to a new cooling rate or to a microstructure it has never seen. Physics-based models handle extrapolation much better, but on their own they are not accurate enough for engineering use. We felt that the best way was to combine the two, with the physics carrying the prediction and the data correcting it, and to be strict about testing every part.

Objectives

The objectives of this project were these.

  1. To build a physically based chain from composition and heat treatment to microstructure, covering the thermodynamics, the heat flow during cooling and quenching, the transformation of austenite and tempering.
  2. To learn the yield strength, tensile strength, elongation and hardness from open data, with machine-learning models that are anchored on this physics.
  3. To answer the question of how furnace cooling and air cooling differ for every category of carbon and low-alloy steel, including the effect of the bar size.
  4. To extend the same chain to hardening, tempering and stress relieving, so that all 5 classic treatments are covered by one model.
  5. To give every prediction a calibrated 90% interval, and to measure how much the results depend on each modelling assumption.
  6. To test every part on data the models never saw, and to deliver the whole study as an open and reproducible tool.

Scope of the Work

The work covers plain carbon and low-alloy steels with up to about 1% C, 2% Mn, 1% Si, 4% Ni, 2% Cr and 1% Mo, in the form of round bars from 10 to 150 mm in diameter, after the 5 treatments of . The tool also recognises steels that lie outside this range, such as stainless, tool and maraging steels, and microalloyed or thermomechanically rolled steels whose strength comes mainly from the rolling schedule, and it tells the user so instead of giving a number it cannot stand behind. Surface treatments such as carburising and nitriding are outside the scope of this project.

Contributions of This Work

We think the project adds the following to what is openly available today.

  • An open chain from composition to properties for all 5 classic treatments, in which the heat flow, the transformation kinetics and the tempering are modelled explicitly and the machine learning only corrects them.
  • A Bayesian calibration of the bainite kinetics on 1040 points of the SAE hardenability bands, sampled with Markov chain Monte Carlo, which also improves independent checks that played no part in the calibration.
  • Property models that are clearly better than the textbook relations on grades they never saw, with a tensile strength error of 47 MPa against 94 MPa for annealed and normalised steel and 75 MPa against 247 MPa for quenched and tempered steel, and with 90% intervals that hold on those unseen grades.
  • A clear and quantitative answer to how furnace cooling and air cooling differ for each category of steel, and how the bar size changes that answer.
  • A register of every modelling assumption with a sensitivity analysis, so that a reader can see which conclusion depends on what.
  • A compilation of 215 measured values from 52 open papers, each traceable to a table in its source, and a reproducible pipeline with 98 automated tests, executed notebooks and an interactive application.
