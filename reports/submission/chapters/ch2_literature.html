Literature Review

The Iron–Carbon System and Critical Temperatures

Every heat treatment of a steel is planned around a few critical temperatures. In the iron–carbon system austenite is stable above the upper critical temperature Ae3, which falls from 912 °C for pure iron to the eutectoid temperature of about 727 °C at about 0.76% C, and below the lower critical temperature Ae1 it gives way to ferrite and cementite . Above the eutectoid composition the upper limit is the cementite solvus, Aecm. Alloying elements move all of these temperatures. Manganese and nickel lower them, while silicon, chromium and molybdenum generally raise the lower critical temperature. For engineering use the critical temperatures are usually estimated with empirical equations, such as those of Grange and Andrews , and the martensite start temperature Ms with relations such as that of Steven and Haynes . Just below the eutectoid temperature, the lever rule gives the equilibrium amounts of proeutectoid ferrite and pearlite.

Annealing and Normalising

In full annealing a hypoeutectoid steel is austenitised a little above Ac3 and cooled with the furnace, typically at 10 to 30 °C per hour through the transformation range. In normalising it is austenitised about 50 to 100 °C above the upper critical temperature and cooled in still air . The 2 routes therefore differ in 2 ways at once, in the austenitising temperature and in the cooling rate, and the second one matters most. Air cooling makes the austenite transform at a larger undercooling, so the pearlite is finer, with an interlamellar spacing that falls roughly as the inverse of the undercooling , and finer pearlite is stronger . The ferrite grains become smaller as well. In hypoeutectoid steels faster cooling can also take the austenite into the extrapolated region below Ae1 before all the equilibrium ferrite has formed, which gives more pearlite than the lever rule predicts .

Hardenability changes this picture for alloy steels. In a steel with enough chromium, molybdenum or nickel, the ferrite and pearlite reactions are delayed so much that bainite or even martensite forms in an air-cooled bar, so normalising such a steel gives a different kind of microstructure altogether. A thicker bar also cools more slowly in air, so the same steel normalised as a thin bar and as a thick bar can end up with different structures and properties, which is the well-known mass effect .

Hardening and Hardenability

Hardening means cooling austenite fast enough that it transforms to martensite. The fraction of martensite that forms below Ms depends mainly on the undercooling and is described well by the equation of Koistinen and Marburger . The hardness of fresh martensite depends mainly on its carbon content, and above about 0.6% C it levels off, because some austenite is retained .

How deep a steel hardens is called its hardenability. It is measured with the Jominy end-quench test, in which a 25 mm bar is quenched from one end by a water jet and the hardness is read along its length , and the SAE hardenability bands give the allowed range of these curves for each H-steel . Grossmann expressed hardenability as the ideal critical diameter DI, the largest bar that reaches 50% martensite at its centre in an ideal quench, which can be estimated from composition and grain size with multiplying factors . Real quenchants are described by the Grossmann severity of quench H, which runs from about 0.3 per inch for still oil to about 1 for still water and up to 5 for violently agitated brine .

Tempering and Stress Relieving

Fresh martensite is too brittle for most uses, so hardened steel is almost always tempered. On reheating, carbon first segregates and precipitates as transition carbides, retained austenite decomposes at around 200 to 300 °C, cementite replaces the transition carbides, and at higher temperatures the cementite coarsens and the matrix recovers, so the hardness falls steadily with temperature and time . In steels with molybdenum, vanadium or chromium, fine alloy carbides precipitate at about 500 to 650 °C and slow down this softening, or even raise the hardness again, which is called secondary hardening. Hollomon and Jaffe showed that temperature and time can be combined into a single tempering parameter, \(P = T\,(C + \log t)\), so that treatments with the same P give about the same hardness , and Grange, Hribal and Porter showed how the hardness of tempered martensite depends on carbon and the alloying elements .

Tempering also has 2 well-known traps. Tempering in the range of about 250 to 400 °C can lower the toughness of martensite, which is called tempered martensite embrittlement, and holding in or slowly cooling through about 375 to 575 °C can embrittle steels that contain nickel, chromium and manganese together with impurities such as phosphorus and tin, which is called temper embrittlement . The susceptibility to temper embrittlement is often judged with the J factor of Watanabe and co-workers .

Stress relieving is a subcritical treatment, done below Ac1 so that the microstructure does not change. During the soak a residual stress cannot stay higher than the yield strength of the steel at that temperature, so it relaxes, first by plastic flow and then by creep. The fall of the yield strength with temperature is tabulated for structural steel in EN 1993-1-2 . For a steel that was tempered before, the stress relief also counts as extra tempering, and Hollomon–Jaffe additivity tells whether it will soften the steel further.

Modelling Phase Transformations

The classical way to predict what forms on continuous cooling is a semi-empirical kinetic model for each reaction, combined with an additivity rule that adds up the fractions of the incubation time spent at each temperature. Kirkaldy and Venugopalan proposed such a model for the ferrite, pearlite and bainite reactions in low-alloy steels , and Li et al. refined it and calibrated it on hardenability data . Collins et al. published an open implementation of the Li model together with dilatometry of 3 steels , which we use as a check of our own implementation. Commercial tools such as JMatPro combine CALPHAD thermodynamics with kinetic and structure–property models . Machine learning has also been used to predict CCT and TTT diagrams directly from composition , and recent work adds physical constraints or physics-based features to such models . These data-driven approaches usually need thousands of diagrams, often from closed databases.

Structure–Property Relationships

For ferrite–pearlite steels, Gladman, McIvor and Pickering gave the yield and tensile strength as a mixture law of ferrite and pearlite, with the ferrite strengthened by its grain size through the Hall–Petch relation and the pearlite by its interlamellar spacing . Pickering also gave the impact transition temperature as a function of the same quantities . Maynier, Jungmann and Dollet gave the hardness of ferrite–pearlite, bainite and martensite as functions of composition and cooling rate , which makes it possible to estimate the hardness of mixed structures. The ferrite grain size after cooling can be estimated with the relations of Hodgson and Gibbs . In microalloyed steels, fine carbonitrides of vanadium and niobium add precipitation strengthening, which can be estimated from their solubility products and the Ashby–Orowan relation .

Machine Learning for Steel Properties

Neural networks have been used to predict steel properties since the 1990s , and later work predicts properties from composition and processing or guides the model with physical metallurgy . With small materials datasets the main risks are extrapolation and over-confidence. Random cross-validation hides how a model does on genuinely new materials , a model should say where it can be trusted , and its intervals should be calibrated, which conformal prediction does without assuming any distribution . Gaussian processes suit small data well because they give an uncertainty with every prediction , and correcting a physical model with a statistical discrepancy term is the idea behind the Bayesian calibration of Kennedy and O'Hagan . Choosing the next experiment where the model is least certain is known as active learning .

Research Gap

We did not find an open model that takes an arbitrary carbon or low-alloy composition through all 5 classic heat treatments to the microstructure and the tensile properties, gives honest intervals, and is tested on measured data at every step. The commercial tools are closed, the open kinetic models stop at the microstructure, and the machine-learning models usually leave out the heat treatment or need far more data than is openly available. This project was designed to fill exactly this gap.
