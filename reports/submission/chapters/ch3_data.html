Data

We used only open data, or factual handbook values that are openly reproduced, and we kept every file together with its source and its licence. One rule we followed throughout is that every record must say how the steel was heat treated, because a strength value without its treatment cannot teach a model anything about heat treatment.

Cleaning and Checking the Data

A few things about the data were important to get right. Handbook typical values and specification minimum values are quite different numbers for the same steel, the minima being about 25 to 35% lower, so we never mixed the two. The composition tables had a few real errors, such as a row shift in the alloy H-steel table, swapped manganese ranges for 1117 and 1118, missing decimal points, and 15B21H printed as 15821H, and we corrected each of them and logged it with its reason. For the literature compilation, every number we transcribed was checked by a script against the saved paper, and values that were physically implausible, such as an annealed 1045 at 56 HV, were kept in the file with a quality flag but left out of every metric.

The Birbilis database needed the same care. It lists many grades twice, 4140 and 4140H for example, with identical numbers, and it also contains stainless, tool and cast steels and carburised and cold-drawn products. After removing these and the repeated rows, its 3234 entries become 324 quenched and tempered bars of 28 grades and 241 normalised or annealed bars, and the parser that does this has its own tests. In this database 240 of the quenched bars were quenched in oil and 84 in water, mostly in 13, 25, 50 and 100 mm sizes. Its compositions are nominal, as in the handbook. The tempering time is not stated, so we used 1 h, and shows that 0.5 h or 2 h changes the results very little.

The Bar Size of the Handbook Data

The handbook gives one normalised value per grade but does not say on what size of bar it was measured, and for normalising this matters, because a thin bar cools faster in air than a thick one. The Birbilis database contains the same handbook values with the bar size written out, so we compared the 2 grade by grade. The match is very clear, as shows. At 25 mm the mean difference in tensile strength is only 0.4 MPa and 21 of the 28 grades agree within 3 MPa, while at the other sizes almost none of them do. All the normalised handbook rows are therefore treated as 25 mm (1 inch) round bars cooled in still air.

How Large Is the Data?

A fair question is why the data of this project are only a few megabytes, when some steel datasets run to a gigabyte. Steel datasets of that size are almost always collections of images, such as the ultrahigh-carbon steel micrograph database with 961 SEM images in about 270 MB , or large tables without the processing history. Neither of them can teach a model what furnace cooling, air cooling or tempering does to the strength of a steel. What decides how well a property model generalises is the number of distinct steels and treatments with a measured property and a known treatment, and in tabular form that is never a large number.

Our data hold about 2,700 such records, from the handbook and Birbilis bars, the Jominy band points, the CCT-atlas points, the dilatometry and the literature values, plus 1358 real heat analyses and 547 composition ranges for the classifier, and every record has its heat treatment. Because the property data cover a few dozen grades, every error in this report is measured on grades that the model never saw during training, which is a much stricter test than a random split.
