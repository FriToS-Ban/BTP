Methodology

Overview of the Model Chain

The model follows the same steps a metallurgist would follow on paper, as shows. From the composition it first decides what kind of steel this is and whether it lies inside the range the model knows. It then computes the critical temperatures, works out how the bar cools for the chosen treatment, and integrates the transformation kinetics along that cooling path to get the phases and how fine they are. Tempering and stress relieving act on this microstructure, the classical structure–property laws turn it into a physics estimate of strength and hardness, and Gaussian-process models anchored on that estimate give the final properties with their intervals. Each step is a separate, tested module, so every step can be checked on its own data, and this is what lets us say where an error comes from when we see one.

Steel Category and Domain Check

To train the steel classifier we simulated 40 heats per grade inside the published composition ranges of 547 grades, which gives 21,880 heats in 13 categories, from low-carbon steel to tool steel. We trained 5 classifiers, which were logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours, a small neural network, a random forest and XGBoost , and tested them on grades they had never seen, by grouping the cross-validation folds by grade, and on 1358 real heats. Before looking at any result we fixed the rule that the production model would be the one with the best accuracy on the real heats. As a fair baseline we also wrote the AISI/SAE composition limits as a simple decision list with nothing fitted, for example S ≥ 0.07% for free-machining steels, Si ≥ 1% for Si-Mn steels and Mo ≥ 0.08% for Mo and Cr-Mo steels, with carbon limits of 0.25 and 0.60% . A novelty score, the distance to the nearest known grade, lets the classifier answer that a steel belongs to none of the categories, and the same check tells the property models when a composition is outside the range they were trained on.

Critical Temperatures and Default Treatments

The equilibrium critical temperatures come from the equations of Grange , which are written in °F and converted to °C in the code,

\mathrm{Ae}_3\ (\text{°F}) = 1570 - 323\,\mathrm{C} - 25\,\mathrm{Mn} + 80\,\mathrm{Si} - 3\,\mathrm{Cr} - 32\,\mathrm{Ni} \mathrm{Ae}_1\ (\text{°F}) = 1333 - 25\,\mathrm{Mn} + 40\,\mathrm{Si} + 42\,\mathrm{Cr} - 26\,\mathrm{Ni}

with the element symbols standing for their contents in wt%. The cementite solvus Aecm comes from the fit used by Collins et al. , the eutectoid point from the intersection of Ae3 and Aecm, and the equilibrium pearlite fraction of a hypoeutectoid steel from the lever rule,

f_P = \frac{\mathrm{C} - 0.02}{\mathrm{C}_e - 0.02}

where Ce is the eutectoid carbon content. The martensite start temperature follows Steven and Haynes ,

M_s\ (\text{°C}) = 561 - 474\,\mathrm{C} - 33\,\mathrm{Mn} - 17\,\mathrm{Ni} - 17\,\mathrm{Cr} \\ - 21\,\mathrm{Mo} - 7.5\,\mathrm{Si} + 10\,\mathrm{Co}

When the user does not give a temperature, the tool uses the practice of its own data. Full annealing is done at the higher of Ae3 + 50 °C and Ae1 + 65 °C, normalising at the upper critical temperature plus 110 °C but not below 870 °C, and hardening at the higher of Ae3 + 70 °C and Ae1 + 90 °C. These simple rules reproduce the temperatures of the handbook and the Birbilis data within 14, 15 and 16 °C on average, and they change smoothly with composition. Compositions outside the window in which the empirical equations were derived are flagged.

Cooling and Quenching

Furnace cooling is treated as Newtonian cooling with a rate of 0.005 K/s at 700 °C. Air cooling is a heat balance of a long round bar in still air, with natural convection, radiation and the temperature-dependent specific heat of steel from EN 1993-1-2 ,

\rho\, c(T)\, \frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{4}{D}\left[ h_c\,(T - T_\infty) + \varepsilon\, \sigma \left(T^4 - T_\infty^4\right) \right] \\ h_c = 1.32 \left( \frac{T - T_\infty}{D} \right)^{1/4}

where D is the bar diameter, ε = 0.8 is the emissivity and σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. The Biot number stays below 0.1 up to a diameter of 100 mm, so the bar can be treated as one lump at a single temperature.

A quenched bar is different, because its surface and its centre cool at very different rates, so for a quench we solve the transient radial conduction in a long round bar with finite volumes,

\rho\, c(T)\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left( k(T)\, r\, \frac{\partial T}{\partial r} \right), \qquad -k \left. \frac{\partial T}{\partial r} \right|_{r = R} = h\,(T_s - T_{\mathrm{bath}})

with the conductivity k(T) of EN 1993-1-2. The heat-transfer coefficient comes from Grossmann's severity of quench H ,

h = 2\, k_{\mathrm{ref}}\, H, \qquad k_{\mathrm{ref}} = 30\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}

using the middle of the mild-agitation class of the Grossmann table, which is 0.325 per inch for oil and 1.05 per inch for water, and an ideal quench holds the surface at the bath temperature. With constant properties the solver matches the exact Bessel-series solution to within 2% of the temperature difference, which is checked by an automated test. During a liquid quench the specific heat is used without the peak that EN 1993-1-2 puts near 735 °C. That peak stands for the latent heat of the ferrite and pearlite reactions during slow cooling, and in a quench the austenite is undercooled far below 735 °C before anything forms, so leaving it out is the physically right choice here. The tensile properties of a quenched bar are predicted at the specimen position of ASTM A370 , which is the centre for bars up to 38 mm and the mid-radius for larger bars.

The Jominy bar is solved by 1-D conduction along its length, with a water-jet coefficient of about 8000 W m−2 K−1 at the quenched end. With this value the cooling rates along the bar match the scale of the combined hardenability charts within a factor of 0.86 to 1.26 over the whole bar.

Transformation Kinetics

The ferrite, pearlite and bainite reactions follow the model of Kirkaldy and Venugopalan as modified by Li et al. , which we wrote from its published equations. The time to reach a fraction X of reaction j at temperature T is

\tau_j(X, T) = \frac{S(X)}{k_j(T)}, \qquad S(X) = \int_0^X \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{0.4(1-x)}\,(1-x)^{0.4x}}

where the rate kj grows with the undercooling below the start temperature of the reaction, falls with the composition-dependent factors FC, PC and BC of Li et al., and depends on the ASTM grain size number G of the austenite. For continuous cooling the reaction is integrated along the cooling path with the additivity rule,

S\big(X(t)\big) = \int_0^t k_j\big(T(t')\big)\, \mathrm{d}t'

which is the Scheil idea written for the whole reaction and not only for its start. Ferrite forms below Ae3 and enriches the remaining austenite in carbon, pearlite starts once that austenite is below both its own Ae3 and Aecm (the Hultgren condition), bainite forms below the bainite start temperature

B_s\ (\text{°C}) = 637 - 58\,\mathrm{C} - 35\,\mathrm{Mn} - 15\,\mathrm{Ni} - 34\,\mathrm{Cr} - 41\,\mathrm{Mo} + \Delta B_s

and the austenite that is left at Ms transforms to martensite according to Koistinen and Marburger ,

f_M = f_\gamma \left[ 1 - \exp\!\big(-0.011\,(M_s - T)\big) \right]

where fγ is the fraction of austenite remaining. The prior-austenite grain size is taken as ASTM 8 after austenitising at up to 850 °C, one number coarser for every 100 °C above that, which is typical of fine-grained, aluminium-killed steel. The kinetics run in compiled code , which makes it practical to sweep every grade of the catalogue over many cooling conditions. shows 2 continuous cooling diagrams computed this way, with the furnace and air cooling paths drawn on them.

Continuous cooling diagrams computed by the model for AISI 1045 and AISI 4140, with the furnace cooling path (0.005 K/s at 700 °C) and the still-air path of a 25 mm bar. The same air cooling gives ferrite and pearlite in 1045 and mostly bainite in 4140.

Calibration on the hardenability bands. We calibrated 4 constants of the kinetics, multipliers on the ferrite, pearlite and bainite rates and the shift ΔBs, on the SAE hardenability bands of the non-boron H-steels, 1040 points in all. The published model is the prior, with the logarithm of each multiplier distributed as N(0, 1) and ΔBs as N(0, 50 °C). The target is the middle of each band, and the noise is a quarter of the band width plus a model error that is fitted together with the constants. The hardness along the bar is computed from the predicted phases with the relations of Maynier et al. . We first found the maximum of the posterior and then sampled the full posterior with 4 adaptive Metropolis chains started from spread-out points, checking convergence with the split R-hat statistic and the effective sample size . Boron grades are left out, because neither the kinetics nor the hardness relations contain boron. We adopted the calibration for the whole chain only after checking that it also improves predictions on data it never saw ().

Microstructure Quantities

The kinetics decide how much bainite, martensite and proeutectoid cementite form. The remaining ferrite and pearlite are reported with their lever-rule split as the central value and the kinetic split as the upper end of a band, which agrees best with measured fractions in normalised steels. The interlamellar spacing of the pearlite follows a power law in the undercooling ΔT below Ae1 at which the pearlite forms,

S_0 = 3.33\, \Delta T^{-0.607}\ \ \mu\mathrm{m}

which we fitted on 14 measured continuous-cooling spacings from 4 papers. The ferrite grain size follows the relation of Hodgson and Gibbs for low carbon equivalents, with the carbon equivalent held at 0.35 at most so that medium-carbon steels do not all fall onto the lower limit of the relation.

Structure–Property Relations

For the ferrite and pearlite part of the structure, the yield and tensile strength come from the mixture law of Gladman, McIvor and Pickering ,

\sigma_y = f_\alpha^{1/3}\left(35.4 + 58.5\,\mathrm{Mn} + 17.4\, d^{-1/2}\right) \\ + \left(1 - f_\alpha^{1/3}\right)\left(178.9 + 3.9\, S_0^{-1/2}\right) + 63\,\mathrm{Si} + 425\sqrt{N_f} \sigma_{\mathrm{UTS}} = f_\alpha^{1/3}\left(246 + 1143\sqrt{N_f} + 18.1\, d^{-1/2}\right) \\ + \left(1 - f_\alpha^{1/3}\right)\left(719 + 3.5\, S_0^{-1/2}\right) + 95\,\mathrm{Si}

in MPa, with the ferrite fraction fα, the ferrite grain size d and the spacing S0 in mm, and the free nitrogen Nf in wt%. For bainite and martensite we use the hardness relations of Maynier et al. , for example for martensite

\mathrm{HV}_M = 127 + 949\,\mathrm{C} + 27\,\mathrm{Si} + 11\,\mathrm{Mn} + 8\,\mathrm{Ni} + 16\,\mathrm{Cr} + 21\log V_r

where Vr is the cooling rate at 700 °C in °C/h, capped at 865 HV because retained austenite stops fresh martensite from getting harder above about 0.6% C . The physics strength that anchors the machine-learning models combines the two parts,

\sigma_{\mathrm{phys}} = f_{FP}\, \sigma_{\mathrm{UTS}}^{\mathrm{Gladman}} + f_{BM} \times 3.3\, \mathrm{HV}^{\mathrm{Maynier}}_{BM}

where fFP and fBM are the fractions of ferrite plus pearlite and of bainite plus martensite. Uniform elongation is reported as a fixed fraction, 0.508 ± 0.051, of the total elongation once both are put on the same 4.5√S0 gauge with Oliver's relation , a ratio we found from 10 tensile tests in 4 papers. For ferrite–pearlite states the tool also gives Pickering's impact transition temperature ,

\mathrm{ITT}\ (\text{°C}) = -19 + 44\,\mathrm{Si} + 700\sqrt{N_f} + 2.2\,P_{\%} - 11.5\, d^{-1/2}

where P% is the percentage of pearlite.

Tempering Model

Tempering is described with the Hollomon–Jaffe parameter ,

P = T_{\mathrm{K}}\, \left(20 + \log_{10} t_{\mathrm{h}}\right)

with the temperature in kelvin and the time in hours. A first treatment with parameter P1 counts at a second temperature T2 as an equivalent time

t_{\mathrm{eq}} = 10^{\,P_1/T_{2,\mathrm{K}} - 20}\ \ \mathrm{h}

which is how 2 treatments add up. Martensite softens from its as-quenched hardness HVM0 towards a fully tempered hardness along a logistic curve in P, and molybdenum, vanadium and chromium add a secondary-hardening bump near 600 °C,

\mathrm{HV}_M(P) = \mathrm{HV}_{\mathrm{soft}} + \frac{\mathrm{HV}_{M0} - \mathrm{HV}_{\mathrm{soft}}}{1 + \exp\!\left[(P - P_{50})/W\right]} \\ + \left(a_{\mathrm{Mo}}\mathrm{Mo} + a_{\mathrm{V}}\mathrm{V} + a_{\mathrm{Cr}}\mathrm{Cr}\right) \exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{P - 17500}{1500}\right)^{2}\right]

where the midpoint of the softening moves linearly with the alloy content,

P_{50} = p_0 + p_{\mathrm{Mn}}\mathrm{Mn} + p_{\mathrm{Si}}\mathrm{Si} + p_{\mathrm{Cr}}\mathrm{Cr} + p_{\mathrm{Mo}}\mathrm{Mo} + p_{\mathrm{V}}\mathrm{V} \\ \mathrm{HV}_{\mathrm{soft}} = s_0 + s_{\mathrm{C}}\,\mathrm{C}

Bainite keeps its hardness until the tempered martensite falls below it and then follows it, and ferrite and pearlite are left unchanged. The 12 constants were fitted on the tensile strengths of the 324 quenched and tempered bars through UTS = 3.3 HV, starting from literature priors. To make the fit well behaved, it works in coordinates scaled by the prior widths and uses several starting points, and a bootstrap over grades gives the uncertainty of each fitted constant.

Stress Relieving, Precipitation and Toughness

Stress relieving. The tool first checks that the soak is subcritical, at least 25 °C below Ac1 from the equation of Andrews . During the soak a residual stress can be no larger than the yield strength at that temperature, so it is bounded by

\sigma_{\mathrm{res}} \le k_y(T)\, \sigma_y

with the reduction factor ky(T) of EN 1993-1-2 , which is 1.0 up to 400 °C, 0.78 at 500 °C, 0.47 at 600 °C and 0.23 at 700 °C. For a tempered steel the soak is added to the earlier tempering with , and the properties change only if the combined parameter is higher than the one the steel already had.

Precipitation strengthening. For steels with vanadium or niobium above microalloying levels, the amounts that dissolve at the austenitising temperature come from the solubility products of Irvine et al. and Narita , and the strengthening from the re-precipitated particles follows the Ashby–Orowan relation ,

\Delta\sigma = \frac{5.9\sqrt{f}}{x} \ln\!\left( \frac{x}{2.5 \times 10^{-4}} \right)\ \ \mathrm{MPa}

where f is the volume fraction of particles and x their mean size in µm, taken as 8 nm after air cooling (fine interphase precipitates) and 30 nm after furnace cooling (coarsened) .

Toughness and composition flags. For tempered states the tool flags the 2 documented embrittlement windows, tempered martensite embrittlement for tempering at 250 to 400 °C and temper embrittlement for 375 to 575 °C in steels with Ni, Cr and Mn, using Watanabe's factor

J = (\mathrm{Mn} + \mathrm{Si})(\mathrm{P} + \mathrm{Sn}) \times 10^{4}

with a residual tin content of 0.01% when none is given. Low-carbon chemistries with Nb, Ti or V and at least 1% Mn are recognised as thermomechanically processed (TMCP) or HSLA types, and boron steels are flagged, so that the user knows when a prediction needs extra care.

Machine-Learning Models

All the property models are Gaussian processes with a Matérn 5/2 kernel that has its own length scale for every input, plus a white-noise term, and the hyperparameters are found by maximising the marginal likelihood from several starting points . The key idea is the anchor. Instead of learning the property from scratch, the physics-anchored model learns only a multiplicative correction to the physics strength,

y(\mathbf{x}) = y_{\mathrm{phys}}(\mathbf{x})\, \exp\!\big[g(\mathbf{x})\big], \qquad g \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(0, k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\big)

so that far from the training data the correction fades to zero and the prediction falls back on the physics. This is a simple form of the discrepancy modelling of Kennedy and O'Hagan .

For annealed and normalised steel the production model averages 3 partly independent routes from microstructure to properties, the physics-anchored chain, the textbook relations with a Gaussian-process residual, and a Gaussian process on composition and physics descriptors. When the cooling produces more bainite and martensite than any training steel had, the weight of the purely data-driven member moves to the 2 physics-anchored members. The microstructure enters the machine learning in the form of the Gladman mixture law, as the ferrite share, the ferrite Hall–Petch term and the pearlite fineness, each weighted by the ferrite plus pearlite fraction, so that these inputs go smoothly to zero for a bainitic steel. Silicon and vanadium are not used as machine-learning inputs, because almost every training grade has the same nominal silicon and almost none has vanadium, so a model that is allowed to use them learns effects that are not real. For quenched and tempered steel the production model is an equal mixture of a physics-anchored Gaussian process and a Gaussian process on the composition and the process variables, with the tempering expressed as the equivalent temperature for 1 h from .

Validation and Uncertainty

Every model was scored by leave-one-grade-out cross-validation, in which all the rows of one grade are left out, the model is trained on the rest and the left-out grade is predicted, so a grade is never in the training set when it is predicted. The 90% intervals are normalised split-conformal intervals ,

\hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) \pm q\, \sigma(\mathbf{x}), \qquad q = \text{the } \lceil (1 - \alpha)(n + 1) \rceil\text{-th smallest of } |y_i - \hat{y}_i|/\sigma_i

with α = 0.1, where σ(x) is the model's own standard deviation and the scores come from the left-out grades, so the coverage of the intervals is itself checked on unseen grades. Models were compared with paired tests on the per-grade errors, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test with the Holm correction and a bootstrap interval of the difference in RMSE. We also checked that the predictions behave physically over sweeps that the data do not contain, such as the cooling rate, the carbon content and the silicon content.

For the transformation model, 200 draws from the posterior of the kinetic constants were pushed through the kinetics to the 423 measured points of the CCT atlas, which played no part in the calibration, to see how much of the error the parameter uncertainty explains and how much has to be added as a model-form term. Finally, every modelling assumption was moved to the ends of its plausible range one at a time, with the models retrained each time, and a Morris elementary-effects screen varied all of them together, so that we know which conclusions depend on which assumption.
