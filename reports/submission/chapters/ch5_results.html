Results and Discussion

Steel Categories

All 5 classifiers learned the 13 categories well on grades they had never seen, with accuracies from 0.84 to 0.93. XGBoost, which the rule fixed in advance selected, reaches 0.921 on unseen grades and 0.942 at the family level on the 1358 real SteelBench heats. The AISI/SAE decision list, with nothing fitted, reaches 0.879 and 0.920, which is a strong baseline, as it should be, since the categories are defined by those limits. The machine-learning model is better where it matters most, on real heats, near the limits, and in recognising steels that belong to no category at all, and all 312 maraging steels of an open steel-strength dataset are flagged as unknown by it, against 58% by the rules. The 2 disagree on 13% of the real heats, almost all of them near a limit such as 1.0% Mn or 0.25% C, and where they agree the family is right 97% of the time, which is why the tool shows both answers side by side. SHAP analysis shows that the elements the classifier relies on are the ones a metallurgist would look at, sulphur for free-machining steels, silicon for spring steels, molybdenum for Cr-Mo steels, nickel for Ni-Cr-Mo steels, carbon for the plain carbon classes, and chromium for almost every class, because its absence is what separates a plain carbon steel from a chromium steel.

The Transformation Model

Start temperatures. Our implementation of the Li model reproduces the published implementation of Collins et al. to within 1 to 2 °C for the ferrite and bainite start, which confirms that the equations are coded correctly. Against the measured dilatometry of EN3B, EN8 and SA-540 at 0.1 to 50 K/s (), the model predicts the ferrite start within 15.7 °C (mean absolute error over 18 cases), the pearlite start within 22.6 °C and Ms within 7.4 °C. With the published constants the bainite start came out 67 °C too high on average, and the calibration on the hardenability bands brings this to −20 °C.

Transformation start temperatures predicted by the model (lines) against the dilatometry of Collins et al. (points) for 3 steels cooled at 0.1 to 50 K/s.

CCT atlas. Against 423 measured continuous-cooling points of 19 low-alloy steels, the model predicts the ferrite plus pearlite fraction within 0.12 (mean absolute error), the bainite fraction within 0.16, the martensite fraction within 0.12 and the hardness within 68 HV (RMSE). For quench-like cooling, with less than 20 s from 800 to 500 °C, the ferrite plus pearlite fraction is within 0.05.

Hardenability bands. With the published constants the model is right at the quenched end of the Jominy bar, with a bias of +0.6 HRC and 85% of the points inside the band for positions J1 to J4, but it is too hardenable further along the bar. The calibration changes only the bainite reaction, lowering Bs by 107 °C and speeding up the reaction below it by a factor of 79, and on grades it never saw (5 folds grouped by grade) the band error falls from 10.1 to 5.9 HRC while the share of points inside the band rises from 54% to 78% ().

Jominy end-quench curves of 9 H-steels from the heat-flow and kinetics model, with the published constants of Li et al. (solid) and after the calibration on the hardenability bands (dashed), against the SAE bands (grey).

Transfer to independent data. A good fit to hardness bands could in principle come from the wrong mechanism, so we checked the calibrated model against data that played no part in the calibration. The bainite start moved onto the dilatometry, from +67 to −20 °C, the Ms error fell from 10.3 to 7.4 °C, the hardness error on the CCT atlas fell from 83 to 69 HV, the pearlite fraction of air-cooled literature steels improved from 21.8 to 19.0 points, and the hardness of the 25 mm normalised handbook bars improved strongly, from 58 to 31 HB. These checks come from 4 different kinds of data that the calibration never saw, and this is the reason we adopted the calibration for the whole chain.

Microstructure

For furnace-cooled steels both the kinetics and the lever rule agree with the measured pearlite fractions, with mean absolute errors of 4.3 and 5.5 points, and across the 13 literature steels with a measured pearlite fraction the reported best estimate is within 7.6 points on average. Normalising refines the ferrite grain size from 38 to 18 µm in low-carbon steels and from 19 to 12 µm in medium-carbon steels as 25 mm bars, and air cooling makes the pearlite spacing less than half of its furnace-cooled value at every carbon content ().

The real value of the physics shows outside the data. On the dilatometry steels cooled at 0.1 to 5 K/s, which are rates and microstructures that the handbook data do not contain, a Gaussian process on composition alone is 109 HB off, while the physics-anchored production model is 68 HB off, because the physics carries it into the bainite and martensite range where a data-only model has nothing to go on. Anchoring on the Gladman law, rather than on a hardness relation for every constituent, was a deliberate choice, and an ablation confirms it, cutting the error at new cooling rates from 73 to 68 HB and the error outside the domain from 114 to 108 MPa. The predictions also behave physically. Over cooling-rate sweeps of all 33 grades the predicted tensile strength moves the wrong way in only 0.5% of the steps, and over a normal silicon range of 0.10 to 0.40% it changes by 26 MPa, close to the few tens of MPa that physics expects.

It is worth asking how much of the remaining error can be removed at all. The handbook gives one value per grade at the nominal composition, but a real heat can sit anywhere inside the specification. Moving each grade's composition inside its own specification range moves the predicted tensile strength by 18 MPa (RMS) after furnace cooling and by 59 MPa after air cooling, where the hardenability varies within a grade. The model's error of 47 MPa is of the same size, so much of what is left comes from the unknown real composition of the tested heats rather than from the model.

Low-carbon steels. Both routes give ferrite with about 15% pearlite, and air cooling only refines it, with finer pearlite and a ferrite grain size of 18 µm instead of 38 µm. So the tensile strength rises modestly, by about 37 MPa, and the ductility stays the same. These steels are normalised to refine the grain and make it uniform rather than for strength, and the predicted impact transition temperature falls by about 26 °C, which is just what normalising is meant to do.

Medium- and high-carbon steels. Here both routes give the same phases, but the pearlite is much finer after air cooling and there is more of it to refine, so the gain grows with carbon, from about 79 MPa for medium-carbon to about 240 MPa for high-carbon steels as 25 mm bars. This is the classic chart of coarse against fine pearlite from the textbooks, now computed from first principles and with intervals ().

Plain carbon steels with 0.75% Mn and 0.25% Si, furnace cooled (blue) and air cooled as 25 mm bars (orange), across the carbon content, with 90% intervals. Grey bands mark carbon contents outside the training data.

Free-machining and C-Mn steels. After furnace cooling these behave like plain carbon steels of the same carbon content, and their manganese delays the ferrite and pearlite enough that a few grades form some bainite in thin air-cooled bars.

Cr, Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo steels. For these steels air cooling gives a different steel altogether. Their hardenability, with a median ideal critical diameter of 71 mm for the Cr-Mo grades, delays the ferrite and pearlite so much that a 25 mm bar forms mostly bainite, and the median tensile strength of the Cr-Mo grades rises from 611 to 741 MPa. Hardenability orders all the grades this way (). Below an ideal critical diameter of about 40 mm almost no 25 mm bar forms more than half bainite and martensite, and above 100 mm almost all of them do. In practice such steels are tempered after normalising, which is consistent with this picture.

Hardenability, as the ideal critical diameter from the ASTM A255 factors , against the bainite plus martensite that forms in an air-cooled 25 mm bar (left) and the gain in tensile strength on air cooling (right), for every catalogue grade.

Why air cooling is stronger. The 2 routes differ in the austenitising temperature and in the cooling rate, and the model can separate the two by air cooling a bar from the annealing temperature. For the medium-carbon steels +84 MPa of the gain comes from the faster cooling and −4 MPa from the hotter austenitisation, so it is the cooling rate that makes the difference, as the theory of expects.

Bar size. For 1020 and 1045 the bar size changes the tensile strength by only some tens of MPa, because the structure stays ferrite and pearlite and only coarsens a little in a thicker bar. For 4140 and 4340 it decides between martensite, bainite and ferrite with pearlite, so for hardenable steels the bar size matters as much as the choice between the furnace and the air.

In one sentence, for low-carbon steels furnace and air cooling give almost the same steel, for medium- and high-carbon steels air cooling gives the same phases with much finer pearlite and much more strength, and for Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo steels it gives a different, bainitic steel, so the route and the bar size have to be chosen together.

Hardening

The quench model gives the centre of a 25 mm bar a cooling rate of 15 K/s at 700 °C in oil and 44 K/s in water, and the centre of a 100 mm bar 3.0 and 6.3 K/s. Feeding these paths through the kinetics gives the as-quenched structure at any position in the bar. As 25 mm bars quenched in oil, 4140 and 4340 reach 82% and 97% martensite with 55 and 58 HRC at the centre, both inside the 53 to 60 HRC range of the J1 position of their SAE hardenability bands , and 5160 and 1080 reach 99% and 86% martensite with about 65 and 63 HRC. Plain 1020 hardly hardens at all in a 25 mm bar, even in water, because its ideal critical diameter is only about 12 mm, which is exactly why such steels are case hardened rather than through hardened. Fresh martensite is too brittle for a meaningful tensile test, so for the as-quenched state the tool reports the microstructure and the hardness, and the Jominy results of are the main test of this part of the chain.

Stress Relieving and Precipitation Strengthening

For a normalised 1045 bar the largest residual stress that can survive a soak at 600 °C is 197 MPa, set by the fall of the yield strength at that temperature, and creep during the soak lowers it further. For a tempered steel the model answers the practical question of how hot a stress relief can be. A 4140 bar tempered at 600 °C for 1 h, with a tensile strength of 998 MPa, keeps 991 MPa after a stress relief at 575 °C but falls to 916 MPa after one at 650 °C, because by Hollomon–Jaffe additivity the hotter soak tempers the steel further. This is the reason for the shop rule of stress relieving at least 25 to 30 °C below the tempering temperature, and the model reproduces it from the physics.

For microalloyed steels the precipitation term adds about 76 MPa for 0.10% V after air cooling, where the particles are fine, and about 32 MPa after furnace cooling, where they coarsen. For niobium it adds only about 10 MPa at 0.03% Nb, because little niobium dissolves at normalising temperatures, and the main benefit of niobium in a normalised steel is a finer grain from the undissolved particles pinning the austenite.

Uncertainty of the Transformation Model

The posterior of the kinetic constants was sampled with 4 adaptive Metropolis chains, and all of them converged, with a split R-hat of at most 1.007 and an effective sample size of at least 454 for every constant. The hardenability bands pin the bainite constants very tightly, leave the ferrite factor close to where its prior put it, since the bands cannot tell how fast ferrite forms, and suggest a slightly slower pearlite reaction, with a factor of 0.72 (90% interval 0.68 to 0.77).

We then pushed 200 posterior draws through the kinetics to the 423 measured points of the CCT atlas, which played no part in the calibration. The spread from the constants alone is small, because the bands constrain them so well, so the tool adds a model-form term estimated from these independent points, with a standard deviation of 0.22 to 0.27 in phase fraction and 66 HV in hardness. With this term the 90% bands contain 91% of the measured ferrite plus pearlite fractions, 90% of the bainite fractions, 92% of the martensite fractions and 92% of the hardness values, so the uncertainty that the tool reports for a microstructure is calibrated on data it never saw.

Sensitivity to the Modelling Assumptions

Every condition the models rely on was moved to the ends of its plausible range, one at a time, with the models retrained and re-scored each time. For the annealing and normalising model, scored on all annealed and normalised rows, the accuracy hardly changes, staying between 59 and 69 MPa, and the direction of the furnace against air difference and the ranking of the grades never change. The furnace cooling rate is the setting that changes the size of the gain most, for example from 87 to 132 MPa for 1045 over the whole range of usual annealing practice, because a slower furnace gives a softer annealed state.

For the quenched and tempered chain the error of the fitted physics stays between 95 and 102 MPa whatever the setting, because the tempering constants are refitted each time and absorb most of the change. The Morris screen, which varies all the physics assumptions together with the fitted constants held fixed, agrees with these results. The air-cooling gain of 1045 is most sensitive to the austenite grain size, the strength of a tempered 50 mm 4140 bar to the Hollomon–Jaffe constant, which a refit of the tempering constants absorbs almost completely, and the martensite fraction of that bar to the severity of the oil quench. So the conclusions of this chapter do not hang on any single assumption, and where an assumption matters, we know by how much.
