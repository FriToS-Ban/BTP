Conclusions and Future Scope

Conclusions

In this project we built an open, physics-informed model that takes a carbon or low-alloy steel through all 5 classic heat treatments, from its composition to its microstructure and its properties, and we tested every part of it on data it never saw. The main conclusions are these.

  1. A chain that follows the metallurgy, with thermodynamics, heat flow, transformation kinetics and tempering, and uses machine learning only to correct the textbook relations, predicts the tensile strength of unseen grades within 47 MPa after annealing and normalising and within 75 MPa after quenching and tempering, against 94 and 247 MPa for the textbook relations alone.
  2. The 90% intervals of the tool are honest. They contain 92.5% of the annealed and normalised values and 89 to 90% of the quenched and tempered values of grades left out of training.
  3. Calibrating the bainite reaction on the SAE hardenability bands, with a full Bayesian posterior, lowers the bainite start by about 107 °C and improves independent predictions of the dilatometry, the CCT atlas and the hardness of normalised bars.
  4. For low-carbon steels furnace cooling and air cooling give almost the same steel, and normalising mainly refines the grain. For medium- and high-carbon steels air cooling gives the same phases with much finer pearlite and a median gain of about 80 and 240 MPa. For hardenable Cr, Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo steels air cooling gives a different, bainitic steel, and the bar size matters as much as the route.
  5. The tempering model, fitted on 324 bars, recovers known metallurgy on its own, the fading effect of carbon on tempered hardness and the strong delay of softening by molybdenum, and reproduces the mass effect across bar sizes of 13 to 100 mm.
  6. The conclusions do not depend on any single modelling assumption. The accuracy changes little and the direction of every effect stays the same when each assumption is moved across its plausible range, and where an assumption changes the size of an effect, the report says by how much.

We think the most useful thing about this work is that it can be trusted in a clear and checkable way. Every number can be traced to its data and its equation, every prediction says how sure it is, and the tool says so when a steel is outside what it knows.

Future Scope

The natural next step is a laboratory test. The study already includes a validation protocol written before any test, with predictions and 90% intervals for common Indian grades, EN3B, EN8, EN9, EN18, EN19, EN24, EN42 and an 8620-type steel, furnace cooled and air cooled as 10 and 25 mm bars. It fixes how they are to be tested, with spark-emission chemistry of the actual bars, a thermocouple in each bar to record the real cooling curve, tensile tests with an extensometer, hardness after removing the decarburised layer, and point counting, grain size and pearlite spacing on a few samples . Because the predictions are fixed in advance, even a handful of samples would be a genuine test of the model, and the same protocol extends naturally to quenched and tempered bars.

Beyond that, the work can grow in a few directions. Micrograph datasets could be used to check the predicted constituents directly from images. The posterior of the kinetic constants suggests a slightly slower pearlite reaction, which can now be adopted across the whole chain. The heat-flow model can be extended from round bars to plates and other shapes, and the same chain can be extended to surface treatments such as carburising and induction hardening. Finally, as more open data with Charpy results and full heat treatments become available, a toughness model for quenched and tempered steel can be added in the same way, with physics, data and honest uncertainty together.
