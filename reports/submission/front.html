Abstract

Heat treatment decides what a steel is really like in service, and the same bar can be soft and easy to machine or hard and strong depending only on how it was heated and cooled. In this project we built an open, physics-informed machine-learning model that takes the composition of a carbon or low-alloy steel, its heat treatment and the size of the bar, and predicts the microstructure that forms and the yield strength, tensile strength, elongation and hardness that follow, each with a 90% interval. It covers the 5 classic treatments, which are annealing, normalising, hardening, tempering and stress relieving.

The model follows the metallurgy step by step. Empirical thermodynamics gives the critical temperatures, a heat balance gives the cooling path of a bar in a furnace, in still air or in a quench, the Kirkaldy–Venugopalan kinetics of Li et al. gives the ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite, a Hollomon–Jaffe model handles tempering and stress relief, and Gaussian-process models anchored on the classical structure–property laws learn what those laws miss. We calibrated the bainite reaction on 1040 points of the SAE hardenability bands with a full Bayesian posterior.

On grades left out of training, the tensile strength of annealed and normalised steel is predicted within 47 MPa, against 94 MPa for the textbook relations alone, and that of quenched and tempered bars within 75 MPa over 324 bars of 28 grades, against 247 MPa. The 90% intervals contain 92.5% and 89% of these unseen values. The model was also tested against dilatometry, 423 points of a continuous-cooling atlas, the Jominy bands of 80 H-steels, normalised bars of other sizes and of new grades and 215 values from 52 papers.

The question set by our supervisor, how furnace cooling and air cooling differ, gets a clear answer. For low-carbon steels the 2 routes give almost the same steel. For medium- and high-carbon steels air cooling gives the same phases with much finer pearlite and a median gain in tensile strength of about 80 and 240 MPa, and for hardenable Cr-Mo and Ni-Cr-Mo steels it gives a different, bainitic steel, so for them the bar size matters as much as the route. The whole study runs again from one command and comes with a Python interface and an interactive application.

Keywords: heat treatment, annealing, normalising, hardening, tempering, stress relieving, carbon and low-alloy steels, transformation kinetics, Gaussian processes, uncertainty quantification
